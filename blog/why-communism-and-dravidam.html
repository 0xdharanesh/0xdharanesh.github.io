Why Communism and Dravidam

Two words get flattened into slogans or insults. That is lazy. Communism, at its best, is a demand that people not be reduced to instruments of profit. Dravidam, at its best, is a demand that dignity not be rationed by caste, language hierarchy, or priestly authority. This is not a party pamphlet. It is an argument for keeping those demands alive — carefully, with history in view.

First: define the words honestly

In classical Marxian thought, communism is not “the government owns everything forever.” It is the horizon of a classless society in which the means of production are socially controlled, wage labour and private appropriation of surplus are overcome, and people contribute according to ability and receive according to need. Stanford’s overview of Marx treats this as an emancipatory project against alienation and exploitation — not a brand name for every authoritarian state that later called itself communist.

Marx’s early writings obsess over alienation: work that should express human creativity becomes a grind that empties the worker. His later critique of capitalism tracks how profit extraction produces crises, surplus value, and a formal “freedom” that coexists with structural unfreedom for those who must sell labour to survive. You can reject his historical predictions and still keep the diagnostic question: who owns the surplus, and what does that ownership do to human dignity?

Dravidam, in Tamil political history, is a family of movements: Non-Brahmin assertion, the Justice Party (South Indian Liberal Federation, from 1916), the Self-Respect Movement (founded 1925 by S. Ramanathan and led by E. V. Ramasamy — Periyar), Dravida Kazhagam (1944), and later electoral Dravidian parties. The moral core was self-respect: refuse caste humiliation, challenge ritual hierarchy, expand women’s dignity, and treat Tamil / Dravidian identity as a ground for equal belonging — not as a ticket to hate individuals.

Ideals and regimes are not the same object. Criticising gulags, one-party repression, or Tamil political corruption does not erase the legitimacy of asking why some lives are priced cheaper than others.

A short timeline of Dravidian assertion

  • 1916: South Indian Liberal Federation (Justice Party) organises Non-Brahmin political representation against upper-caste capture of colonial administration and opportunity
  • 1925–1926: Self-Respect Movement founded (1925); Periyar takes leadership; Kudi Arasu becomes a key organ
  • 1929 onward: Self-Respect conferences; Self-Respect marriages without priestly monopoly and dowry theatre; women as active organisers
  • 1930s: stronger socialist language (including Tamil serialisation/engagement with the Communist Manifesto in Kudi Arasu); caste framed as protecting economic inequality
  • Late 1930s: anti-Hindi agitation crystallises mass Dravidian linguistic politics
  • 1938–1944: Periyar elected Justice Party president (29 Dec 1938); Justice Party / Self-Respect currents reorganised as Dravida Kazhagam in 1944
  • Later: electoral Dravidian parties take state power and translate fragments of that common-sense into reservations, welfare, and public provisioning — imperfectly

Why communism still names a real problem

Markets coordinate information brilliantly. They also concentrate power. When ownership of land, capital, data, and opportunity hardens into inheritance, “freedom” becomes the freedom of unequal starting lines. The communist critique still bites where:

  • Labour is disposable while capital compounds across generations
  • Public goods — health, education, housing — are treated as residual charity instead of shared infrastructure
  • Formal equality (“everyone may compete”) pretends that centuries of exclusion never happened
  • Platform and data monopolies recreate rentier power in digital clothing

You do not need to endorse every Marxist claim about history or strategy to accept the diagnostic point: an economy that treats workers as cost centres will keep producing dignity deficits unless politics pushes back. Many socialists today frame the minimal shared claim more modestly — that the bulk of production should be under social, democratic control precisely because capitalism’s defects are structural, not personal.

History’s warning is also real. Twentieth-century states that claimed the communist label often built surveillance, famine risk, and elite party privilege. Leninist state ownership is not identical to Marx’s association of free producers — a distinction scholars of socialism keep insisting on. That is not a footnote. It means any living egalitarian project must defend civil liberties, pluralism, independent unions, and exit rights as non-negotiable — otherwise “emancipation” becomes another hierarchy with red branding.

Why Dravidam still names a real problem

Caste did not retire when the Constitution banned untouchability. Contemporary evidence keeps finding material and symbolic gaps:

  • NFHS-based analysis (2019–21) still finds significant caste gaps in education, occupation, and wealth across SC–ST, OBC, and “Others” groups — evidence that traditional hierarchy has not simply dissolved into class.
  • Research on symbolic discrimination against Dalits finds that high-prejudice contexts track slower poverty reduction and poorer relative standing for Dalits over recent decades.
  • School-level data show persistent caste segregation patterns in Indian schooling — isolation is not only “in the past.”

That is why Self-Respect politics still reads as modern, not antique. Periyar’s movement attacked caste endogamy, priestly gatekeeping, and gendered obedience; it experimented with Self-Respect marriages; and it fused anti-caste struggle with Tamil linguistic and cultural assertion — especially after compulsory Hindi policies ignited mass protest in the late 1930s.

Communism meets caste: why class alone was never enough here

In the Indian context, Ambedkar’s critique complements Marx rather than simply repeating him. Caste, he argued, is not only a division of labour — it is a division of labourers, freezing people into birth-assigned work and blocking the free association a class politics assumes. A “working class” that will not annihilate caste cannot become a revolutionary community of equals; it reproduces graded inequality inside the struggle itself.

Periyar converged on a related point from the Dravidian side. He treated caste as an obstacle to socialism and argued that economic restructuring without breaking Brahminical social power would leave the levers of hierarchy intact. In the 1930s Self-Respect publications engaged communist texts; later he told communist friends to confront caste discrimination before imagining a clean communist transition. The bridge between communism and Dravidam, historically, was not accidental cosplay. It was a shared suspicion of inherited domination — with Dravidam insisting that status justice comes before, or at least alongside, class redistribution.

Ambedkar and Periyar were not identical: Ambedkar worked through constitutional democracy and national institutional design; Periyar emphasised mass social reform, rationalism, and Dravidian political identity. They still shared a hard core: Hindu social order as a sanctifier of inequality, and annihilation of caste as non-negotiable.

What the “Dravidian model” actually claims

Political-economy scholarship on Tamil Nadu (notably Kalaiyarasan and Vijayabaskar’s The Dravidian Model) argues that populist mobilisation against caste-status inequality helped democratise access to the modern economy and sustain comparatively stronger human-development outcomes — food, health, education, poverty reduction — rather than waiting for growth to “trickle” into fairness.

Comparative work has made a related point: Tamil Nadu and Gujarat once had similar human-development scores; with similar later growth, Tamil Nadu’s more egalitarian public ideology and administrative capacity tracked a more balanced human-development path. Public-health writing on the “Tamil Nadu model” credits political commitment, rural health infrastructure, drug logistics, and gender-aware service delivery — while still admitting unfinished business: malnutrition, private-sector inequality, and rising expectations.

Reservations in education and jobs fit this logic: not charity, not a claim that talent is caste-distributed, but a corrective for unequal starting points. Ending the remedy before ending the disease is theatre.

Credit the pattern without mythologising parties. Dravidian electoral politics has patronage, personality cults, incomplete Dalit inclusion in capital, and policy failures. Intermediate-caste power can reproduce new hierarchies. The useful claim is narrower: status-equality politics can build better public capacity than growth-only narratives.

Where the two ideas meet — and where they must disagree with their own myths

Communism (as critique) asks who owns the surplus. Dravidam (as critique) asks who is allowed to stand upright. Together they refuse a common lie: that hierarchy is natural, and that the humiliated should be grateful for crumbs.

  • Material fairness without dignity can still leave caste humiliation intact
  • Identity pride without redistribution can become empty theatre while opportunity stays gated
  • Either without democracy becomes another priesthood — red or saffron or khaki
  • Anti-caste universalism should not collapse into anti-person bigotry; critique systems, do not invent new permanent enemies by birth

So “why communism and Dravidam” is not “why copy a dead regime” or “why worship a logo.” It is why keep pressure on exploitation and graded inequality at the same time.

A living checklist, not a catechism

  • Defend reservation and affirmative access while caste outcomes remain unequal
  • Treat public education and health as dignity infrastructure, not leftovers after GDP celebrations
  • Keep labour rights, cooperatives, and democratic ownership debates alive where capital concentrates
  • Refuse communal shortcuts that replace anti-caste solidarity with majoritarian belonging
  • Measure movements by whether the weakest person in the room becomes safer — not by slogan volume
  • Hold parties and cadres to the same anti-humiliation ethic they preach
  • Defend free speech, due process, and minority safety even when “your side” is winning

I care about these traditions because they name unfinished work: people should not be priced by birth, and work should not be a quiet theft of someone else’s future. History complicates every flag. It does not erase the wound those flags were raised to mark. Hold the ideals. Audit the parties. Keep the pressure.
